Asymptotic theory of inference, by hand

Companion to the notes on Chapter 7 of Amari's Information Geometry and Its Applications. Back to the notes. Everything here uses one running example: the curved exponential family $S=\{N(u,u^2)\}$, a Gaussian whose standard deviation equals its mean, sitting inside the two-parameter Gaussian family. A sample of $N$ observations becomes a single point, the observed point $\bar\eta=(\bar x,\overline{x^2})$ in the plane of the means of $x$ and $x^2$, and an estimator is a rule sending that point to a point of $S$. Simulations draw $(\bar x,\overline{x^2})$ exactly, from its true joint law, not samples. The numbers match code/asymptotics.py up to Monte Carlo error.

1. The Cramér–Rao bound and its fine print

For the mean of an exponential distribution the sample mean is unbiased and its variance is exactly the Cramér–Rao bound. For the rate $\lambda=1/\text{mean}$ no estimator attains the bound at finite $N$: the MLE $1/\bar x$ is biased and its variance exceeds the bound by $N^3/((N-1)^2(N-2))$, which is $1+4/N+\dots$; even the unbiased estimator loses a factor $N/(N-2)$. 2. The observed point is a random point near the model

$N$ observations of $N(u,u^2)$ give a point $\bar\eta=(\bar x,\overline{x^2})$ in the plane of $\eta=(\mathbb E x,\mathbb E x^2)$. The model $S$ is the parabola $\eta_2=2\eta_1^2$ (black); the dashed parabola $\eta_2=\eta_1^2$ is zero variance and nothing lies below it. Theorem 7.3: with $\tilde e=\sqrt N(\bar\eta-\eta)$ the mean is $0$, the covariance is the Fisher matrix $g_{ij}=\operatorname{Cov}[(x,x^2)]$ (the ellipse, 1 and 2 standard deviations), and the third moments are $T_{ijk}/\sqrt N$: a skew that dies like $1/\sqrt N$. Push $N$ down to 5 and the cloud visibly leans away from the model; at $N=400$ it is an almost perfect ellipse.

3. An estimator is a foliation of the plane

An estimator sends the observed point to a point of $S$. The set of observed points sent to the same $u$ is a leaf $A(u)$ (the book's ancillary submanifold). Consistent: the leaf through $\eta(u)$ is labelled $u$ (Theorem 7.4 (1)). Efficient: the leaf meets $S$ at a right angle in the Fisher metric. The efficiency of a consistent estimator is $\sin^2$ of the angle between its leaf and $S$, the Schur complement $\bar g/g_{uu}$ of (7.58), and the sampling distribution of $\sqrt N(\hat u-u)$ is asymptotically $N(0,1/\bar g)$. The MLE has straight leaves $\eta_2=u\eta_1+u^2$ (it is the m-projection); the e-orthogonal estimator has parabolic leaves; the sample mean has vertical leaves, $\sqrt{m_2/2}$ horizontal ones. $1.1\,\bar x$ has leaves that miss the true point and is inconsistent: its histogram is centred away from $0$.

4. The bias is not a tensor: the same estimator in five charts

The MLE of a quantity $w$ is simply $w$ evaluated at the observed point. In the $\eta$ chart that is exactly unbiased. In any other chart $w(\eta)$ the bias is the second-order delta-method term, and the book's (7.54) says it equals $-\tfrac1{2N}\,g^{\beta\gamma}\Gamma^{(m)\alpha}_{\beta\gamma}$: the m-connection of the chart contracted with the inverse Fisher metric. The m-connection vanishes in $\eta$, so there is no bias there and a bias elsewhere, and the bias depends on the chart exactly as a Christoffel symbol would (Chapter 5). Pick a point $(\mu,\sigma)$ of the full Gaussian family and an $N$: the table compares the formula, computed numerically from the chart, with a simulation.

5. Second order: where the $1/N$ term comes from

Theorem 7.5: for a bias-corrected efficient estimator, $N\,\mathbb E(\hat u^*-u)^2=g^{uu}+\tfrac1N c+O(N^{-2})$ with $c=\tfrac12\{(\Gamma^m)^2+2(H^e)^2+(H^m_A)^2\}$. The three squares are the parametrisation of $u$ (the m-connection of $S$ in the chart $u$), the e-curvature of the model $S$ (Efron's statistical curvature) and the m-curvature of the leaves. The MLE has straight leaves, so its third term vanishes; the e-orthogonal estimator adds it. Reparametrising by $\log u$ or $u^2$ changes only the first term. Run the simulation: measured against predicted $1/3+c/N$.

6. Hypothesis testing: efficiency is the angle of the boundary

To test $H_0:u=1$ against $u>1$, reject when the observed point is beyond a boundary line in the $\eta$ plane with normal $n$, that is when $n\cdot(\bar\eta-\eta(1))$ is large. For local alternatives $u=1+\delta/\sqrt N$ the power is $\Phi(\delta\,s(n)-z_\alpha)$ with $s(n)=n\cdot\eta'/\sqrt{n^{\mathsf T}Gn}$. By Cauchy–Schwarz $s\le\sqrt{g_{uu}}$ with equality exactly when $n\propto\theta'(1)$, the boundary orthogonal to $S$ (Theorem 7.7: this is the score test). A boundary at another angle loses the fraction $1-\cos^2$ of the information, as an estimator would. Turn the boundary and compare with the best test; the simulation draws the exact distribution of $\bar\eta$ and calibrates each test to the stated level.

7. Outside the regular case: the uniform location family

For $x\sim U[u-\tfrac12,u+\tfrac12]$ the Fisher information is infinite. The midrange converges at rate $1/N$, much faster than $1/\sqrt N$ of the sample mean, and $N(\text{midrange}-u)$ tends to a Laplace law $e^{-2|x|}$ with variance $\tfrac12$ and excess kurtosis $3$: not Gaussian. Two independent copies added have excess kurtosis $1.5$, so the limit is not a stable law either (the book says it is).
